If you've started looking into selling your business, you've probably come across the term "EBITDA multiple" fairly quickly. It's the most common method used to value small and mid-sized businesses in Australia, and understanding it will help you make sense of any offer or valuation you receive.

## What EBITDA actually means

EBITDA stands for Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation and Amortisation. In plain terms, it's a measure of how much cash profit a business generates from its core operations, before accounting for financing decisions, tax position, and non-cash accounting items.

Buyers use EBITDA rather than net profit because it strips out factors that vary from owner to owner — how a business is financed, what tax structure it uses, and how its assets are depreciated — leaving a clearer picture of the underlying operating performance. This makes it easier to compare one business against another.

For many small businesses, EBITDA is calculated by starting with net profit and adding back interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation, along with any "add-backs" — owner's expenses run through the business, one-off costs, or above-market wages paid to family members. A good accountant can help normalise these figures so they reflect the business's true, ongoing earning capacity.

## What a multiple is

A multiple is simply a number that EBITDA is multiplied by to estimate the value of the business. If a business generates $300,000 in EBITDA and sells on a multiple of 3, the implied value is $900,000.

Multiples vary enormously — generally somewhere between 1x and 10x for small and mid-sized businesses in Australia, though both lower and higher multiples do occur in particular circumstances. The multiple reflects how much a buyer is willing to pay for each dollar of ongoing earnings, which in turn reflects how much risk they see in those earnings continuing.

## What drives a multiple higher

A number of factors tend to push multiples up:

- **Lower reliance on the owner.** A business that runs well without the owner's daily involvement is far less risky to a buyer than one where the owner is the business. Strong management and documented processes command a premium.
- **Diversified customer base.** If no single customer represents a large share of revenue, the business is less vulnerable if one relationship ends.
- **Recurring or contracted revenue.** Subscription models, retainers, rent rolls and long-term contracts are valued more highly than one-off project work, because future revenue is more predictable.
- **Growth trajectory.** A business with a track record of steady growth, and clear room to keep growing, is more attractive than one that is flat or declining.
- **Industry and sector sentiment.** Some sectors attract more buyer interest and capital at any given time, which can push multiples up across the board, independent of any individual business's performance.
- **Quality of financial records.** Clean, well-organised financials reduce perceived risk and due diligence time, both of which buyers are willing to pay for.

## What pushes a multiple down

The same factors work in reverse. Heavy owner dependence, customer concentration, inconsistent or declining revenue, messy bookkeeping, and industries facing structural challenges will all tend to compress the multiple a buyer is willing to pay.

Size also matters. Very small businesses — particularly those with EBITDA under a few hundred thousand dollars — often attract lower multiples than larger, more established businesses, partly because there's a smaller pool of buyers with the capital and interest to acquire them, and partly because smaller businesses are often more dependent on a single owner.

## Revenue multiples and other methods

EBITDA multiples aren't the only valuation method. Some businesses — particularly very early-stage or fast-growing technology companies without consistent profit — are valued on a multiple of revenue instead. Asset-heavy businesses, such as those with significant property, plant or equipment, are sometimes valued closer to the value of their net assets, with earnings considered separately. Real estate agencies and similar businesses are often valued using a multiple applied specifically to rent roll income or recurring management fees, rather than overall EBITDA.

The right method depends on the nature of the business, and it's worth discussing with your accountant which approach is most relevant to yours.

## Getting a general sense of value

While a proper valuation should always involve your accountant, or a professional valuer for larger or more complex transactions, it can help to get a general, ballpark estimate early on — if only to sense-check any offer you receive.

A simple way to do this is to multiply your annual EBITDA by a multiple appropriate to your industry and size. It's a rough exercise, not a formal valuation, but it's a useful starting point before any conversation with a buyer.

## A note on expectations

It's natural for owners to feel their business is worth more than the market may ultimately pay — after all, you've put years of work into building it. Multiples are set by what buyers are actually willing to pay in the current market, not by the effort that's gone into a business. Understanding how multiples work, and what specifically drives them in your industry, is the best way to set realistic expectations and negotiate from an informed position.
