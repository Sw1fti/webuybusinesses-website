For many Australian business owners, engaging a broker feels like the default way to sell. It's the path most commonly talked about, and brokers do provide a genuine service for some sellers. But it isn't the only path, and it isn't always the best one. Selling directly to a buyer is increasingly common, particularly for owners who already have a good sense of their business's value and want more control over the process.

Here's an honest look at what changes when you sell without a broker.

## The most obvious difference: fees

Business broker commissions in Australia typically range from around 5% to 10% of the sale price, sometimes with a minimum fee regardless of the final figure. On a business selling for $1 million, that can mean $50,000 to $100,000 going to the broker rather than to you.

Selling directly removes this cost. That doesn't mean a direct sale is free — you'll still want a good accountant and lawyer involved — but their fees are typically a small fraction of what a broker's commission would be, and they're usually charged at a fixed or hourly rate rather than a percentage of the sale price.

## What a broker actually does

To weigh up whether to use one, it helps to be clear on what you're paying for. A good broker typically:

- Markets the business to a pool of potential buyers
- Screens inquiries and filters out unserious ones
- Manages confidentiality during the process
- Helps position and present the business
- Negotiates on your behalf
- Coordinates the moving parts of the transaction

If you don't have an existing buyer in mind and have no way of reaching one, this access to a buyer pool can be valuable. It's the main reason brokers exist.

## When selling direct makes the most sense

Selling direct tends to work well when:

- **You already have a buyer or small number of interested parties** — for example, a competitor who has previously expressed interest, an operator who approaches you directly, or a group actively acquiring businesses in your sector.
- **Your business is relatively straightforward to explain** — the value proposition, financials and operations are easy for a buyer to understand without extensive marketing material.
- **You value confidentiality** — a smaller number of people see your financials and are aware you're considering a sale, which matters if you're concerned about staff, customers or competitors finding out prematurely.
- **You want more control over pace and communication** — dealing with a buyer directly means fewer parties relaying information back and forth, which often speeds up decision-making.

## What to watch for if you go direct

Selling without a broker shifts more responsibility onto you, so it's worth being realistic about the gaps you'll need to fill:

**You'll need good advisers.** An experienced accountant and a lawyer who has handled business sales before are essential. They won't run the whole process for you the way a broker might, but they'll protect you on the two things that matter most: the numbers and the contract.

**You're responsible for qualifying the buyer.** Without a broker screening inquiries, it's on you to ask direct questions early — about their funding, their experience, and their timeline — before sharing sensitive information.

**Negotiation can feel more personal.** A broker acts as a buffer during negotiations, which some sellers find useful emotionally, particularly if price discussions get difficult. Selling direct means you're having those conversations yourself, though many owners find this manageable, especially with a lawyer or accountant backing them up on specifics.

**You won't have access to a wide buyer pool unless you already have one.** If you're selling direct because a buyer approached you, this isn't a concern. If you're starting from scratch with no interested parties, you may need another way to find a buyer — which is where buyers who actively seek out businesses to acquire, like us, can fit in.

## A middle ground

Some owners use a hybrid approach: engaging a broker for a short, defined period to test the market, while also remaining open to direct approaches. Others ask their accountant, who may already have relationships with buyers or other business owners, to make a few discreet introductions. There's no single right answer — it depends on how much time you have, how specialised your business is, and how confident you feel managing the conversations yourself.

## The bottom line

A broker isn't inherently good or bad — they provide a real service in exchange for a real fee. The question is simply whether that service is worth the cost for your situation. If you already have interest from a credible buyer, or you're comfortable engaging directly with one, selling without a broker can save a substantial amount of money and give you more control over how the sale unfolds.
