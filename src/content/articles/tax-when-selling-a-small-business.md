Tax is one of the most important — and most misunderstood — parts of selling a business. The amount you ultimately keep from a sale can vary significantly depending on how the sale is structured, what concessions you may be eligible for, and decisions made well before you ever list the business. This article introduces the main concepts you're likely to encounter, in general terms. It isn't tax advice, and specific rules, rates and thresholds change over time — always confirm current detail with a registered tax agent or accountant before making decisions.

## Capital gains tax: the starting point

When you sell a business, any gain on the sale is generally subject to capital gains tax (CGT) as part of your income tax. In broad terms, the gain is the difference between what you sell the business (or its assets) for, and its original cost base, adjusted for certain factors over time.

CGT applies differently depending on how the business is structured — whether you operate as a sole trader, through a company, a trust, or a partnership — and whether you're selling the business's assets or the entity that owns them (for example, selling shares in a company rather than the business's individual assets). This structural question alone can have a significant effect on the tax outcome, which is why it's worth discussing early, ideally before you're deep into negotiations with a buyer.

## Small business CGT concessions

Australia has a set of small business CGT concessions designed to reduce the tax paid when eligible small business owners sell. These concessions can be valuable, but they come with specific eligibility rules relating to factors such as the value of your business assets, your turnover, how long you've owned the business, and your age or circumstances at the time of sale.

Because eligibility criteria are detailed and can change, this is an area where general reading isn't a substitute for a conversation with your accountant. They'll be able to assess whether you qualify, which concessions might apply, and how to structure the sale to make the most of them — ideally well in advance of going to market, since some planning opportunities are only available before a sale is agreed, not after.

## Timing of the sale

The financial year in which a sale is completed, and the structure of payment (a lump sum versus payments spread over time, for example), can both affect your tax position. Some owners choose to time a sale around other events in their financial life — such as retirement, other income changes, or superannuation contributions — to manage their overall tax outcome. This is highly individual, and your accountant is best placed to model different scenarios based on your specific situation.

## Earn-outs and deferred consideration

Some business sales include an earn-out — part of the purchase price that depends on the business achieving certain results after the sale, often paid over one or more years. Earn-outs can introduce additional complexity from a tax perspective, because the tax treatment of deferred or contingent payments isn't always straightforward. If a buyer proposes an earn-out structure, it's worth having your accountant review the tax implications before you agree to terms, not after the contract is signed.

## GST considerations

The sale of a business can potentially involve GST, although a sale that qualifies as the sale of a "going concern" may be treated differently under current rules. Whether your sale qualifies, and what documentation is required, depends on the specifics of the transaction and should be confirmed with your accountant or tax agent as part of structuring the deal.

## Superannuation strategies

Some business owners use proceeds from a sale to make superannuation contributions, which can be an effective way to manage both retirement savings and tax outcomes, subject to contribution caps and eligibility rules. This is another area where the fine detail matters and changes over time, so it's worth planning with your accountant well before settlement, since some contribution opportunities relate to the timing of the sale itself.

## Why structure matters before you negotiate

A recurring theme across all of the above is that many of the most valuable tax outcomes depend on decisions made before a sale is agreed — not after. Restructuring a business, documenting eligibility for concessions, or planning the timing of a sale are all much harder (and sometimes impossible) to do retroactively once a contract is signed.

If you're even considering a sale in the next year or two, it's worth having an early conversation with your accountant specifically about the tax implications, separate from any conversations you have with a potential buyer. This gives you time to put the right structure in place and head into negotiations with a clear understanding of what you'll actually keep from any given offer.

## A general note

Tax law is detailed, individual, and subject to change, which is why this article deliberately avoids stating specific rates, thresholds or recent changes. Please treat this as a general introduction to the concepts involved, not a substitute for advice from a registered tax agent or accountant who understands your specific circumstances.
