When owners start thinking seriously about leaving their business, it helps to step back and look at the full range of exit options available, rather than assuming there's only one path. Each option suits different circumstances, goals and timeframes. This article compares the most common ones.

## Trade sale

A trade sale means selling the business to another party — often a competitor, a larger business in a related industry, or an investor or operator actively acquiring businesses in your sector.

**Pros:** Trade sales often deliver the strongest financial outcome, since buyers aren't constrained by family dynamics, and strategic buyers may value the business more highly if it complements their existing operations. The process can also move relatively quickly once a suitable buyer is found, and a clean handover is often possible.

**Cons:** Finding the right buyer takes effort, whether through a broker, your own networks, or buyers who approach you directly. Confidentiality needs careful management, particularly if potential buyers include competitors. The culture or direction of the business may also change more than it would under family or management succession.

**Best suited to:** Owners prioritising financial outcome and a relatively clean exit, without a family member or manager ready to take over.

## Management buyout (MBO)

In a management buyout, one or more existing managers purchase the business, often with the help of external finance, seller financing, or both.

**Pros:** The buyers already understand the business, its customers, and its operations, which tends to make for a smoother transition and lower risk of disruption. It also rewards and retains people who have helped build the business.

**Cons:** Managers may not have sufficient capital to pay full market value upfront, which can mean accepting a lower price, a longer payment timeline, or retaining some ongoing financial interest in the business. Not every manager who is good at running day-to-day operations is equally prepared to take on ownership and the risks that come with it.

**Best suited to:** Owners with capable, trusted managers who are interested in ownership, and who are comfortable with a structure that may involve deferred or staged payments.

## Family succession

Passing the business to a child or other family member keeps it within the family and can provide a strong sense of continuity and legacy.

**Pros:** Preserves relationships with long-serving staff and customers who know the family. Can be structured flexibly, including gradual transitions of responsibility and ownership over time. Often carries significant personal and emotional value beyond the financial outcome.

**Cons:** Family succession can create complicated dynamics, particularly if multiple children are involved, or if the chosen successor lacks genuine interest or capability. It can also deliver a lower financial outcome than a trade sale, since family arrangements are rarely negotiated at arm's length.

**Best suited to:** Owners for whom continuing the family legacy matters as much as, or more than, maximising sale price, and where a willing and capable successor exists.

## Selling direct to a buyer (no broker)

This isn't a different type of buyer so much as a different way of reaching one — dealing directly with a trade buyer, an investor, or an operator, without a broker as intermediary.

**Pros:** Avoids broker fees entirely, which can represent a substantial saving. Often allows for faster, more direct communication and negotiation.

**Cons:** Requires the owner to take on more of the process themselves — qualifying buyers, managing confidentiality, and negotiating — ideally with good support from an accountant and lawyer. Works best when a credible buyer already exists or can be found without needing broad market exposure.

## Private equity or investor acquisition

Some businesses, particularly those with strong growth potential or scalable models, attract interest from private equity firms or other investor groups, sometimes through a full acquisition and sometimes through a partial buy-in that retains some owner involvement.

**Pros:** Can provide access to capital and expertise to help the business continue growing, and may allow the owner to retain some ongoing involvement and upside, rather than a complete and immediate exit.

**Cons:** These transactions often involve more complex deal structures, extended due diligence, and ongoing obligations or performance targets if some ownership is retained. They tend to suit larger or higher-growth businesses more than smaller owner-operated ones.

**Best suited to:** Larger businesses, or those with strong growth trajectories, where the owner is open to a partial or staged exit rather than a single clean sale.

## Winding down or asset sale

In some cases, particularly for smaller businesses heavily tied to the owner, an outright trade sale may not be realistic. An alternative is winding down operations and selling individual assets — equipment, stock, premises, or client lists — separately.

**Pros:** Can be simpler to execute than a full business sale, particularly if there's no ready market for the business as a going concern.

**Cons:** Typically delivers a lower total return than selling the business as an operating entity, since it doesn't capture the value of goodwill, customer relationships, or future earning potential.

**Best suited to:** Very small or highly owner-dependent businesses where no realistic buyer exists for the business as a whole.

## Weighing up your own situation

There's rarely a single "best" exit option in the abstract — the right choice depends on your financial goals, your timeframe, whether family or management succession is realistic, and how much you value continuity versus a clean break. Many owners also find it useful to explore more than one path at once; for example, having a conversation with a potential trade buyer while also assessing whether a manager might be interested, before committing to a single direction.
