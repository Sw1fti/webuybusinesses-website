Selling a business is one of the biggest decisions many Australian owners will ever make. For most people, it only happens once. Unlike selling a house, there's no standard process everyone follows, and the right approach depends heavily on your industry, your size, and why you're selling in the first place.

This guide walks through the typical stages of a sale in plain language, so you know roughly what to expect before you start.

## 1. Get clear on why you're selling

Buyers — and their advisers — will ask why you're selling, so it helps to be clear on this yourself first. Common reasons include retirement, health, wanting a change, or simply having taken the business as far as you want to. None of these need to be hidden or dressed up. A straightforward, honest answer tends to build more trust than a vague one.

Your reason for selling can also shape timing. If you're selling because you're ready to retire in the next year or two, that's a very different conversation to selling opportunistically because you've had an approach from an interested buyer.

## 2. Get your financials in order

Whoever buys your business — whether a private buyer, a competitor, or an investment group — will want to understand its financial performance, usually over the last three years. At a minimum, this generally means:

- Profit and loss statements
- Balance sheets
- Tax returns
- A breakdown of any add-backs (personal expenses run through the business, one-off costs, and so on)

If your bookkeeping has been inconsistent, or your accountant hasn't produced management accounts for a while, it's worth tidying this up before you go to market. Clean financials don't just make the business easier to value — they also make the entire process faster, because buyers spend less time asking follow-up questions.

## 3. Understand roughly what the business is worth

Most small and mid-sized businesses in Australia are valued using a multiple of earnings, usually EBITDA (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation). The multiple applied depends on the industry, the size of the business, how reliant it is on the owner, and current market conditions.

It's worth getting a general sense of value before you start any conversations, so you can assess whether an offer is reasonable. Our [valuation calculator](/valuation-calculator) can give you a rough, general estimate based on your EBITDA and an appropriate multiple range — though any serious valuation should also involve your accountant or a professional valuer, particularly if a large amount of money or a complex structure is involved.

## 4. Decide how you want to sell

There are a few common paths:

- **Selling direct to a buyer**, without a broker, where you deal with the buyer yourself or through your accountant or lawyer.
- **Using a business broker**, who markets the business, manages inquiries, and typically charges a commission on the sale price.
- **A trade sale to a competitor or larger operator** in your industry, who may already understand the value of what you've built.
- **Succession to family or management**, where the business stays within a trusted circle.

Each path has trade-offs around cost, speed, confidentiality and control. We cover the differences between broker and direct sales in more detail in [Selling Without a Broker](/resources/selling-without-a-broker).

## 5. Prepare an information pack

Serious buyers will want more than just a one-page summary. A typical information pack includes:

- An overview of the business, its history and what it does
- Key financials and any add-backs
- Information on staff, customers, suppliers and contracts
- Details of assets, leases, and any equipment included in the sale
- An explanation of what's driving the sale

You don't need glossy marketing material. A clear, honest, well-organised pack is far more valuable to a buyer than a polished but vague one.

## 6. Qualify buyers before sharing detail

It's reasonable to ask early questions of anyone who expresses interest — what sort of business they're looking for, whether they have funds available, and what their timeframe is — before sharing sensitive financial detail. Many owners also use a simple confidentiality agreement at this stage, particularly if a competitor could be among the inquiries.

## 7. Negotiate heads of agreement

Once a buyer is serious, the next step is usually a non-binding heads of agreement (sometimes called a letter of intent) that sets out the proposed price, structure and key terms. This isn't the final contract, but it gives both sides something concrete to work from before lawyers get involved and before the buyer commits to due diligence costs.

## 8. Due diligence

This is where the buyer verifies everything you've told them — financials, contracts, leases, compliance, and anything else relevant to the business. It can take anywhere from a few weeks to a few months, depending on the complexity of the business. Being organised at this stage, with documents ready to go, tends to keep deals moving and reduces the chance of a buyer getting cold feet over delays.

## 9. Contracts and legal review

Your lawyer (and the buyer's) will prepare and review the sale contract, covering price, payment terms, warranties, restraint of trade clauses, and settlement conditions. This is a stage where it's worth investing in good legal advice — a poorly drafted contract can create problems long after settlement.

## 10. Settlement and handover

At settlement, ownership transfers and funds are released, often with some amount held in escrow against warranties. Many sales also include a handover period, where the outgoing owner helps transition relationships with staff, customers and suppliers. The length of this varies — anywhere from a couple of weeks to several months — depending on how the business runs and how involved the owner currently is.

## A final thought

Selling a business usually takes longer than owners expect, and the number of moving parts can feel overwhelming at first. Breaking it down into these stages — financials, valuation, preparation, qualifying buyers, negotiation, due diligence, and settlement — makes it far more manageable.
