For many business owners, succession isn't just a financial decision — it's a personal one. Whether you're planning to hand the business to a family member, a trusted manager, or simply want options when the time comes, succession planning works best when it starts early and is approached deliberately, rather than left until circumstances force the issue.

## Why succession planning gets put off

It's common, and understandable, for succession planning to be delayed. Running a business leaves little time for thinking about an eventual exit, and conversations about succession can feel premature, uncomfortable, or emotionally loaded — particularly within families. Some owners also simply haven't decided what they want their life to look like after the business, which makes it hard to plan toward a specific outcome.

The difficulty is that succession planning tends to work best over a long timeframe — often three to five years or more — because it usually involves developing people, restructuring responsibilities, and sometimes restructuring ownership, all of which take time to do well. Starting late doesn't make succession impossible, but it does narrow your options.

## Clarifying your goals first

Before thinking about who the business might go to, it's worth getting clear on what you actually want from the process. Useful questions to consider include:

- Do you want to stay involved in some capacity after you step back, or make a clean break?
- Is continuing the business under the next generation's or a chosen successor's control important to you, separate from the financial outcome?
- How important is maximising the financial return, compared with other goals like fairness among family members, or looking after long-serving staff?
- What's your realistic timeframe — are you thinking years away, or is this something you want resolved soon?

There's no universally right answer to any of these. The point is to be honest with yourself about what matters most, because it shapes every decision that follows.

## Common succession paths

**Family succession.** Passing the business to a child or other family member is the path many owners picture first. It can preserve a family legacy and provide continuity for staff and customers, but it also requires honest assessment of whether the successor genuinely wants the business and has, or can develop, the skills to run it. Family succession that proceeds out of obligation rather than genuine interest and capability often struggles.

**Management buyout.** Selling to one or more existing managers can work well when you have capable people already running parts of the business, and when they have — or can arrange — the finance to buy in. This path often allows for a gradual transition, since the buyers already understand the business.

**Trade sale.** Selling to another business in your industry, or to an investor group, is often the most straightforward option if you don't have a family member or manager ready and willing to take over. It can also deliver the strongest financial outcome, since external buyers aren't constrained by family dynamics or existing relationships in the way an internal succession can be.

**A combination.** Some owners use a hybrid approach — for example, selling a majority stake to an external buyer while retaining a smaller share, or structuring a sale to management with some seller financing involved. These arrangements can balance financial and continuity goals, but they add complexity and are worth structuring carefully with professional advice.

## Preparing the business, not just the successor

Succession planning often focuses heavily on finding and developing the right successor, but the business itself also needs to be prepared. This generally means reducing how dependent the business is on you personally — documenting processes, building a capable team, and ensuring key relationships with customers and suppliers aren't solely tied to you. A business that runs well without the current owner is not only easier to hand over, it's also more valuable, whether the eventual path is family succession, a management buyout, or a trade sale.

## Common pitfalls

A few issues come up repeatedly in succession planning:

- **Leaving it too late**, which limits the options available and can force a rushed decision.
- **Choosing a successor based on obligation rather than readiness**, which can put both the business and the relationship at risk.
- **Not involving an accountant or lawyer early enough**, particularly around structuring, tax implications, and fair treatment of other family members not involved in the business.
- **Avoiding the conversation altogether**, which often leads to confusion or conflict later, especially within families.
- **Underestimating the emotional side**, both for the owner stepping back and for everyone else affected by the transition.

## Getting started

If you haven't formally started succession planning, a reasonable first step is simply to have an honest conversation — with yourself, and eventually with family members, key staff, or advisers — about what you want the next chapter to look like, and over what timeframe. From there, involving your accountant and a lawyer experienced in succession matters will help you understand the practical options available and start preparing the business accordingly.
